Hybrid search blends two complementary retrieval methods: a keyword search (e.g., BM25) that excels at exact matches for rare terms, code snippets, or product IDs, and a semantic (vector) search that captures broader, natural‑language queries. The two result sets are combined by a fusion layer—commonly Reciprocal Rank Fusion (RRF)—which merges rankings to leverage the precision of keyword matches and the coverage of semantic similarity before passing the context to the LLM [11_keyword_search.txt]. Document chunking is a prerequisite for effective RAG because LLMs have limited context windows and embedding models perform best on focused, cohesive passages. Breaking large documents into smaller chunks improves embedding quality (preventing dilution of meaning), enhances retrieval precision (allowing the engine to pinpoint exact sentences or paragraphs), and ensures the retrieved chunks fit within the model’s context window alongside prompts and chat history [12_document_chunking.txt].

Assistant Answer:
Reciprocal Rank Fusion (RRF) merges the result lists from the different search methods by looking at the rank position of each document in each list rather than the raw relevance scores. For each document it computes a score that is the sum of 1 / (k + rank) across the lists (where *k* is a constant, commonly 60) and then re‑orders the combined list by these summed scores. Because it relies only on rank positions, RRF works well when the underlying searches (e.g., BM25 keyword search and vector‑based semantic search) produce scores on different scales, allowing the two sets to be fused without needing to normalize the scores [07_hybridserach.txt, 02_rag_info_retrieval.txt].

Vector database design must balance several architectural trade‑offs. The key factors are (1) **Recall vs. latency** – higher ANN index parameters improve accuracy but increase query time; (2) **Memory vs. cost** – keeping the full index in RAM gives sub‑10 ms latency but raises cloud costs, while disk‑based options (e.g., DiskANN) reduce cost at a modest latency penalty; (3) **Filter performance** – applying metadata filters during the vector traversal (pre‑filtering) is faster than post‑filtering after retrieval; and (4) the overall pipeline components such as ingestion (chunking, embedding), storage, indexing, and query execution shape performance and scalability【13_vector_database_design.txt】.

Choosing between them depends on cost, desired dimensionality, and the specific downstream tasks.
